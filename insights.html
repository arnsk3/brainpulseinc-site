The 18 Months That Will Decide AI Governance

Between June 2026 and December 2027, three regulatory frameworks ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ the EU AI Act, ADA Title II, and OMB M-24-10 ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ will converge into a single compliance reality for any organization deploying AI in regulated environments. This is the roadmap.

The convergence problem

For most of the past decade, AI governance was a theoretical exercise. White papers, ethics frameworks, NIST consultations, voluntary commitments. Important work, but rarely consequential. That is now ending. Between now and the close of 2027, three regulatory regimes ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ each significant on its own ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ will become simultaneously enforceable across the U.S. federal government, state and local governments, the European Union, and any organization with material U.S. or EU operations. The intersection of those three regimes creates a compliance reality that almost no organization is currently prepared for.

The standard advice ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ "appoint an AI ethics committee," "publish a responsible AI charter" ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ was sufficient when these frameworks were aspirational. They are no longer aspirational. They are now sources of enforceable obligations with fines, debarments, and private rights of action attached. The work ahead is engineering work, not policy work.

EU AI Act: enforcement begins August 2026

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act is the world's first comprehensive horizontal AI regulation. Adopted in March 2024, it entered into force on August 1, 2024. The compliance cascade is staggered: prohibitions on unacceptable-risk AI took effect February 2025; general-purpose AI obligations followed in August 2025; high-risk AI requirements arrive August 2026; and the framework reaches full enforcement by August 2027.

For most enterprises, August 2026 is the operative date. That is when the bulk of substantive obligations ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ risk management systems, data governance, technical documentation, transparency requirements, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, conformity assessments ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ become enforceable for high-risk AI systems. The penalty ceiling is ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ¬35 million or 7% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher, for the most serious violations. For context: that is higher than GDPR's ceiling.

The trap most U.S. organizations are falling into is assuming "we don't do business in Europe" is a sufficient answer. The EU AI Act applies extraterritorially in three scenarios: (1) the provider places the AI system on the EU market regardless of where the provider is established; (2) the deployer of a high-risk AI system is located in the EU; (3) the output of the AI system is used in the EU. The third trigger is broad ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ if your AI assists a decision that affects an EU resident, you may be in scope.

Practical implication
Every enterprise with material AI deployments should perform a Union-applicability analysis before August 2026 ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ not as an abstract legal exercise, but as a documented inventory mapping each AI system against the four risk tiers (Unacceptable, High, Limited, Minimal) and the extraterritoriality triggers. Most organizations discover they have at least one in-scope system they had not categorized.

ADA Title II: deadlines April 2026 and April 2027

In April 2024, the U.S. Department of Justice issued a final rule under the Americans with Disabilities Act establishing that WCAG 2.1 Level AA is the binding accessibility standard for web content and mobile applications operated by state and local governments. The compliance deadline is staggered by jurisdiction size: large public entities (50,000+ people served) must comply by April 24, 2026; smaller entities (under 50,000) have until April 26, 2027.

For state and local agencies, this is the most significant accessibility regulation since Section 508. Every customer-facing digital service ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ benefits portals, court systems, transit applications, permit systems, public records interfaces, school district platforms ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ must meet WCAG 2.1 AA. The enforcement mechanism includes private rights of action, meaning lawsuits from disability rights organizations and individual plaintiffs are inevitable.

For private-sector organizations, ADA Title II is not directly applicable ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ but the case law is converging. Federal courts in the Ninth, Third, and First Circuits have ruled that Title III of the ADA (governing public accommodations) applies to websites and apps. The plaintiffs' bar has industrialized ADA lawsuits: 4,605 federal lawsuits were filed in 2023 alone ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ a 300% increase over five years. WCAG 2.1 AA is becoming the de facto private-sector standard regardless of formal regulation.

OMB M-24-10: federal AI requirements active now

In March 2024, the Office of Management and Budget issued Memorandum M-24-10, "Advancing Governance, Innovation, and Risk Management for Agency Use of Artificial Intelligence." This is the operative document for federal AI deployment. Every CFO Act agency must designate a Chief AI Officer, publish an AI use case inventory, conduct algorithmic impact assessments for rights-impacting and safety-impacting AI, implement minimum risk management practices, and maintain transparent public reporting.

For federal contractors, the cascade is immediate. M-24-10 requirements flow through procurement vehicles. AI systems delivered to federal agencies must satisfy the agency's M-24-10 obligations, which means contractors need: documented algorithmic impact assessments, demonstrable risk mitigation controls, human oversight mechanisms appropriate to the use case, and ongoing monitoring with incident reporting. The new AI Bill of Materials (AI BoM) requirement is the AI-era equivalent of the Software Bill of Materials mandated after SolarWinds.

The convergence point: AI BoM is required by OMB M-24-10. The EU AI Act requires similar supply chain transparency. ISO/IEC 42001 embeds these as managerial requirements. By 2027, a single AI BoM artifact will satisfy obligations across all three regimes ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ if it's built correctly.

The eighteen-month roadmap

If you operate AI systems in any of these regulated contexts ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and most enterprises now do ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ the 18 months between June 2026 and December 2027 should be structured around four phases:

Months 1-3 (Inventory and triage). Build the AI use case inventory. Classify every system against EU AI Act risk tiers, OMB M-24-10 categories (rights-impacting, safety-impacting, neither), and ADA Title II scope if applicable. Identify gaps. This is also the phase to designate accountable executives ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ Chief AI Officer for federal contexts, equivalent senior owner for private sector.

Months 4-9 (Build the governance infrastructure). Stand up the risk management system, the documentation pipeline, the AI BoM generator, the model card library, the human oversight controls, and the audit trail visualization. For most organizations, this requires both new tooling and process changes ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ the tooling without the process changes is theater, the process changes without the tooling are unsustainable.

Months 10-15 (Conformity assessments and remediation). Run formal conformity assessments against EU AI Act high-risk requirements. Conduct algorithmic impact assessments under M-24-10. Validate WCAG 2.1 AA conformance for any in-scope digital services. Remediate findings. Generate compliance evidence packages.

Months 16-18 (Ongoing operations and reporting). Transition from project mode to operational mode. Quarterly model card reviews, continuous drift monitoring, annual AIA refreshes, incident response drills, regulatory horizon scanning. This is the phase that most organizations skip ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and that determines whether the previous 15 months of work survives the first organizational change, leadership transition, or model retraining cycle.

What this means for engineering teams

Two implications. First, the artifacts that will be required ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ model cards, AIAs, AI BoMs, TEVV reports, audit trails, oversight UIs ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ are not document templates. They are software systems that must be built, deployed, and maintained. Most organizations underestimate the engineering work by an order of magnitude. Second, the cross-cutting requirement that connects EU AI Act, M-24-10, ADA Title II, and the underlying NIST AI RMF is traceability ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ the ability to demonstrate that every design decision, model behavior, and operational outcome can be linked to a documented basis. Building for traceability now is dramatically cheaper than retrofitting it after a regulator asks the question.

The 18 months ahead are when AI governance stops being a topic and starts being a function. The organizations that build the function deliberately will spend the next decade compounding the advantages. The organizations that wait until they receive their first notice of inquiry will spend the next decade managing the consequences.

Why Most AI Dashboards Fail Their First Compliance Audit

A tactical critique of the model card, TEVV report, and audit trail interfaces shipping today ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and the engineering-grade rebuilds that pass NIST AI RMF, EU AI Act, and OMB M-24-10 evidence requirements.

The dashboard theater problem

I have reviewed dozens of AI governance dashboards over the past 18 months ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ built by Fortune 500 internal teams, AI consultancies, design agencies, and the major cloud platforms. Almost all of them share the same structural flaw: they look like they should pass an audit, but they don't. The visualizations are beautiful. The KPIs are crisp. The color palettes are tasteful. And when an auditor opens a model card or asks "show me the evidence trail for this AIA decision," the interface collapses into a series of placeholder text fields, screenshots from PowerPoint, and "documentation will be available in the next release" footnotes.

This isn't a knock on the designers. It's a knock on the framing. Most AI dashboards are built as executive reporting tools ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ designed to make AI governance look good in a board meeting. They're not built as evidence-of-conformity systems ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ designed to satisfy a regulator who needs to verify, for example, that the algorithmic impact assessment was completed by appropriately trained personnel, reviewed by the relevant authority, and updated within the required cadence.

The audit-readiness gap shows up at three specific points: model cards, TEVV reports, and audit trails. Each is worth analyzing in detail.

Failure mode 1: Model cards as marketing collateral

The model card pattern, introduced by Mitchell et al. in 2018, has now been adopted across the industry. Almost every model card I review includes the same fields: model name, intended use cases, performance metrics, training data summary, known limitations. These fields are necessary. They are nowhere near sufficient.

What an EU AI Act conformity assessor or an OMB M-24-10 auditor actually wants from a model card:

  • Data lineage with timestamps and authorities. Not just "trained on a curated dataset" ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ which dataset, sourced when, from where, under what license, validated by whom, signed off on by what authority.
  • Performance metrics with confidence intervals across protected attributes. Not just "92% accuracy" ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ accuracy with what threshold, on what test set, with what subgroup breakdown, validated against what fairness criteria.
  • Versioning and change history. Not just the current model ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ the lineage from version to version, what changed, why it changed, who approved it.
  • Linked evidence artifacts. Every claim on the model card should be linked to the document, dataset, or test result that substantiates it.
  • Reviewer authority and date. Who reviewed this model card, what is their authority to do so, when was the last review, when is the next required review.

The engineering rebuild is straightforward: every field on the model card has a "show evidence" link. The link goes to the underlying artifact ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ the training data manifest, the test result CSV, the fairness assessment report, the reviewer's digital signature with timestamp. The model card becomes a navigation surface into the evidence corpus, not a summary that lives in isolation.

Design pattern
Treat the model card as the index page of a hyperlinked evidence library. Every metric is a link. Every claim is a citation. The model card itself is short ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ under one screen ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ because its job is to direct auditors to the underlying evidence, not to present the evidence inline. This is closer to a Wikipedia article structure than a marketing brochure.

Failure mode 2: TEVV reports as static screenshots

The Test, Evaluation, Verification, and Validation (TEVV) reporting category is where NIST AI RMF gets most operational. TEVV reports answer the questions: is this model performing as expected? Has it drifted? Are bias metrics within tolerance? Has performance degraded since deployment? These are continuous questions, asked on production data, with answers that change over time.

The pattern I see most often: TEVV reports presented as quarterly PDFs with screenshots of monitoring dashboards. This is the worst of both worlds. The PDFs are out of date by the time they reach the auditor. The screenshots are not interactive, not queryable, not linkable to underlying data. And the cadence ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ quarterly ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ is misaligned with how drift actually behaves, which is continuous and sometimes sudden.

What works instead: TEVV as a live operational surface, with automated alerts feeding both engineering teams and compliance teams. Specifically:

  • Continuous drift monitoring with statistical thresholds documented in advance. When drift exceeds the threshold, an alert fires automatically and an event is logged with timestamp, magnitude, and reviewer assignment.
  • Bias monitoring across protected attributes on every batch of production inferences. Per-subgroup performance is tracked over time, and divergence between subgroups triggers escalation.
  • Performance degradation alerts when accuracy, precision, recall, or domain-specific metrics drop below pre-registered acceptable ranges.
  • Incident logging with full event chain ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ when did the issue start, who was alerted, what investigation occurred, what remediation was applied, when was it closed.
  • Quarterly executive summaries auto-generated from the live data, with one-click access from any summary back to the source events.

The audit posture changes completely. Instead of "here is our quarterly TEVV PDF from three months ago," the answer becomes "here is the live TEVV dashboard, here is the incident log, here is the audit trail for any incident you want to investigate."

Failure mode 3: Audit trails as log dumps

The audit trail is the most consequential surface in any AI governance interface ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and the most consistently neglected. When a regulator asks "show me what happened in the period from January to March," the audit trail is what answers the question. When an inspector general investigates a specific AI-assisted decision, the audit trail is what gets subpoenaed. When a Congressional committee asks for an evidence package, the audit trail is what gets produced.

Most audit trail interfaces I review are essentially log dumps ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ chronological tables of events with raw timestamps, IDs, and event codes. They are technically complete and operationally useless. A reviewer cannot easily filter to the events that matter, cannot reconstruct the sequence of a specific decision, cannot export an evidence package, cannot link events back to source artifacts.

The engineering rebuild requires treating the audit trail as an investigative tool, not a log file. Specifically:

  • Faceted filtering by AI system, decision type, time range, outcome category, reviewer, severity
  • Decision-level reconstruction ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ given a single AI-assisted decision, surface the full chain: input data, model version, inference, confidence, human review, override, final outcome, downstream effects
  • Linked source artifacts ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ every event in the audit trail links to the underlying data, document, or model version
  • Evidence package export ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ one-click generation of a portable evidence bundle for any time range or decision sequence, with cryptographic integrity verification
  • Access logging ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ the audit trail itself is audited; every query against the audit trail is logged

This is where the engineering effort is greatest, and where the audit-readiness gap is widest. Most organizations have not built audit trails of this fidelity, because they haven't been asked to produce them ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ yet. They will be. The 18-month window discussed in the previous article ends with exactly these requests.

The single test

If you want to evaluate your own AI governance dashboard against an audit-readiness benchmark, run this exercise: pick one AI-assisted decision your system made in the last 90 days. From the dashboard, can you produce ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ in under 15 minutes ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ a printable evidence package containing the input data, the model version and its full model card, the inference details, any human review events, the final outcome, and the basis for the decision? If yes, your governance interface is operating at audit-readiness. If no, you have engineering work to do ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and you have until mid-2027 to do it.

Accessibility Is Not a Constraint. It's a Specification.

The framing problem at the root of most accessibility failures ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and what changes when accessibility becomes an architectural specification embedded in design tokens, CI/CD pipelines, and organizational governance.

The framing that fails

In nearly every organization I have advised on accessibility ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ from federal agencies to Fortune 100 retailers ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ the first conversation reveals the same framing problem. Accessibility is treated as a constraint. A box to check. A requirement that limits what the design team can do. A burden the engineering team has to accommodate. A risk the legal team wants to manage. Sometimes the framing is more sympathetic ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ accessibility as a moral obligation, accessibility as inclusivity ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ but the structural position is the same. Accessibility sits outside the design and engineering work, applied as a filter after decisions have been made.

This framing produces predictable failure modes. Design files are reviewed for contrast issues at the end of the design cycle, when fixing them requires reworking the visual system. Components are audited for keyboard navigation after they ship, when fixing them requires breaking changes for downstream consumers. Screen reader compatibility is tested by a small accessibility team after release, when fixes require regression testing the entire surface area. Each of these failure modes is expensive. Each is preventable. None of them is prevented by treating accessibility as a constraint.

The reframing

The reframing that actually works: accessibility is a specification. The same architectural class as performance specifications, security specifications, or browser compatibility specifications. Specifications are not filters applied after the fact. Specifications are inputs to the design process. They constrain what counts as a valid solution from the beginning, and they make invalid solutions impossible to ship through normal channels.

When accessibility is a specification, the conversation in the design review changes. The question stops being "is this accessible?" and becomes "does this meet our accessibility specification?" The first question invites debate, judgment calls, and the negotiation of exceptions. The second question has an objective answer ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ yes or no ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ that can be tested automatically.

1. Accessibility is a design token

Color, typography, spacing, motion ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ these are design tokens. They are encoded in code, consumed by components, and propagated through the system automatically. When a designer wants to use a non-standard color, they cannot. The token is the specification.

Accessibility properties belong in the same token system. Contrast ratios are tokens ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ not heuristics applied after the fact. A "primary action" token specifies the minimum contrast ratio against its background, and the token value is derived to satisfy WCAG 2.2 AAA (7:1) by construction. Focus indicators are tokens with specified outline widths, offsets, and colors that maintain visibility against any background. Touch targets are tokens with specified minimum sizes (44px ÃÂÃÂ 44px per WCAG 2.5.5) baked into the spacing system. Motion is a token with reduced-motion fallbacks specified at the design system level, not implemented per component.

When accessibility is a token, the question "is this accessible?" becomes "did this component consume its tokens correctly?" ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and the answer is testable in CI/CD.

2. Accessibility is a CI/CD pipeline

The most powerful organizational change I have seen at any client is embedding accessibility validation into the CI/CD pipeline. Not as a manual audit gate. As an automated check that runs on every pull request, against every component, in the same execution path as type checking and unit tests.

The tooling exists. axe-core integrates into testing frameworks and catches a meaningful percentage of WCAG violations automatically. Pa11y handles continuous monitoring of deployed environments. Custom tools like SSA11y can be tuned to organization-specific component patterns. The combination catches more violations than manual auditing ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and catches them before they reach a reviewer, an auditor, or a user with a disability.

What changes when accessibility is a pipeline: the cost of fixing a violation drops by an order of magnitude. The violation is caught at the moment it's introduced, by the engineer who introduced it, with full context on what changed. The fix is small, local, and trivially verifiable. Compare this to the cost of catching the same violation in a quarterly accessibility audit: the engineer has moved on, the context is lost, the fix requires regression testing, and the audit cycle introduces a 90-day delay before remediation begins.

Implementation note
Most teams I have advised can install axe-core into their existing test pipeline in under a day. The work that takes longer is configuring which rules are blocking versus advisory, training engineers on how to interpret violations, and triaging the backlog of existing violations the new pipeline immediately surfaces. Plan 4-8 weeks for the full rollout. Plan to never go back.

3. Accessibility is an organizational specification

Tokens and pipelines are necessary but not sufficient. The systemic shift requires organizational specifications ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ formal policies that establish accessibility as an architectural property of the products you ship, owned by named executives, with defined consequences for non-compliance.

The specifications that matter are mostly unglamorous: a policy that no production deployment can ship with axe-core violations of severity "critical" or "serious"; a policy that VPATs are updated and published on every release cycle; a policy that designs are reviewed against the accessibility token system before development begins; a policy that accessibility violations discovered post-deployment are treated as production incidents with defined response SLAs.

These policies are dull to write and consequential to enforce. They are also the difference between an organization where accessibility is "a thing we care about" and an organization where accessibility is an architectural property of every product. The former gets sued. The latter does not.

The compounding effect

The reframing ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ from constraint to specification ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ has a compounding effect that pays off over years. Every component built under the new specification is accessible by default, forever. Every engineer trained under the new pipeline catches accessibility issues automatically for the rest of their career. Every product team operating under the new organizational policy starts new initiatives with accessibility as an input, not an audit.

This is what scaled accessibility looks like at organizations like the Social Security Administration, where SSA11y deployed across 12 application modules serving over 70 million Americans ÃÂ¢ÃÂÃÂ and at Best Buy, where the inclusive design program moved the organization from "we'll get to accessibility eventually" to "no production code ships with critical violations." The shift took two years of organizational work. It will compound for decades.

Accessibility is not a constraint. It is a specification. The organizations that internalize the difference will compound the advantage. The organizations that don't will be in court.
